The Editorial

Who Gives a Damn?

By Zhan Petrov · Updated November 12, 2026

Air Force One was headed for Beijing in May when Secretary of State Marco Rubio warned Donald Trump aboard the plane that U.S. intelligence had linked NVIDIA’s chief executive to a suspected Chinese spy, according to The New York Times and The Washington Post. The plane stopped for fuel in Anchorage. Jensen Huang got on.

Six months later, at a November 9 White House press conference, a reporter asked Trump if he knew about it at the time. “No, I didn’t hear about it then,” he said. According to the Times, Rubio’s warning was at least the second time Trump had been briefed since October 2025. Apparently the president’s memory had a longer layover than the plane.

Who gives a damn who sleeps with whom? Leave Huang’s private life—his November 8 apology memo to employees admitting marital infidelity and a subsequent Times report of a separate relationship with an NVIDIA executive who reported directly to him—to the tabloids and the HR lawyers. Follow the money and the hardware instead.

Who paid? Where did the servers land? Who checked?

Start with the accounting paradox The Wall Street Journal highlighted around Singapore-based Megaspeed International, NVIDIA’s largest Southeast Asian cloud partner. In the fiscal quarter ended July 2025, NVIDIA booked $10.2 billion in revenue—22 percent of its global total, up 81 percent year over year—to customer billing addresses in Singapore. Yet NVIDIA’s own Form 10-Q footnote acknowledged that customers used Singapore to centralize invoicing while hardware was “almost always shipped elsewhere,” attributing over 99 percent of export-controlled data-center compute billed there to orders from U.S.-based customers. One quarter later, after placing Megaspeed on an internal block list amid intensifying scrutiny, NVIDIA switched its geographic disclosure from customer billing address to customer headquarters. Singapore vanished as a breakout line overnight.

Spun out of a Chinese gaming group’s Singapore arm in 2023 under Alice Huang (Huang Le) before being sold that autumn to a Singapore company owned by James Tan Yong Pong—and crowned an official NVIDIA Cloud Partner days later—Megaspeed scaled at astonishing speed. Analyzing customs and trade import records from 2023 through November 2025, Bloomberg found that Megaspeed International and its Southeast Asian arms imported at least $4.6 billion of NVIDIA AI hardware, roughly 136,000 GPUs shipped via server partners—more than a thousand times the firm’s 2023 cash balance. When U.S. Commerce Department Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) inspectors checked Johor sites run by Megaspeed’s Malaysian unit, Speedmatrix, they found NVIDIA gear still sitting in unopened boxes, The Information reported.

Then came NVIDIA’s own tour of the server rooms. After NVIDIA’s general counsel contacted BIS investigators in August 2025 citing their superior’s permission to discuss the probe—prompting agents to hand the inquiry to the Justice Department—NVIDIA visited 12 data centers owned by eight companies across three Southeast Asian countries, sent inventory checks to BIS, and assured Bloomberg in mid-November 2025 that all the hardware was racked where it belonged. Bloomberg’s audit of trade data showed otherwise: NVIDIA’s tally to Washington had undercounted partner-sold chips to Megaspeed by 50,000 units, worth billions. Pressed in December 2025, NVIDIA said it had checked additional Megaspeed warehouses holding the missing gear without disclosing how many chips were inside.

The headline billion-dollar totals measure two different scopes over the same 2023–2025 window. Bloomberg’s $4.6 billion counts customs-recorded NVIDIA hardware imports (~136,000 GPUs) by Singapore-based Megaspeed International and its Southeast Asian affiliates from 2023 through November 2025. The Information’s at least $7.5 billion (~240,000 restricted H100, H200, A800, and H800 GPUs across 2023–2025) widens the lens across three connected corporate legs: that $4.6 billion imported through Megaspeed in Singapore, roughly $2 billion more routed through Megaspeed’s Malaysian subsidiary Speedmatrix, plus restricted GPUs bought from other distributors by Shanghai Century Litong—a mainland Chinese data-center operator founded in June 2023 under a shareholder controlled by Alice Huang’s sister, granted NVIDIA Preferred Cloud Partner status that October, and renting compute inside China to firms including Alibaba and Tencent.

Megaspeed denies breaking any rules. Reposting its December 2025 rebuttal on November 9, the company accused Bloomberg of “misleading information and incorrect insinuations,” said inspections by U.S. and Malaysian authorities as well as NVIDIA found no evidence of illicit chip transfers, and answered “No” when asked whether Alice Huang was a spy. (Beijing’s Foreign Ministry rejected the espionage allegation as a groundless smear, and on November 12 U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche said neither the Justice Department nor the FBI had reached a conclusion on whether she worked for Chinese intelligence.) On November 12, Megaspeed CEO and Chairman James Tan Yong Pong said the firm takes U.S. export-control laws “extremely seriously,” cooperated with BIS inspections, and hired an American law firm to conduct a “comprehensive census” of its GPUs.

Untangling those competing claims means separating three things that AI balance sheets routinely blur: billing address, physical delivery, and compute rental. A billing address only records which office tower receives the invoice—which is how $10.2 billion of quarterly orders could sit under Singapore in an SEC filing while crates almost never unloaded at Singapore’s port. Physical delivery is where the servers actually land and stay: bolting restricted GPUs into racks in Johor, Malaysia can comply with U.S. rules if the cards stay put, whereas diverting physical hardware into mainland China violates export controls. Compute rental is a third layer altogether: under U.S. rules, an offshore operator in Malaysia could legally lease remote cloud access to restricted GPUs to Chinese customers such as Alibaba even while shipping the physical chips into China was banned—whereas Shanghai Century Litong renting controlled GPUs inside mainland China raised the harder question of how the silicon crossed the border.

Nor does routing restricted AI hardware through third-country middlemen require a C-suite entanglement. In a separate intermediary pipeline documented by Bloomberg in October 2024, Mumbai-based pharmaceutical company Shreya Life Sciences exported 1,111 Malaysia-origin Dell PowerEdge XE9680 servers carrying restricted AI processors, including NVIDIA H100 chips—roughly $300 million of hardware between April and August 2024—to two unsanctioned Russian trading firms, legally under Indian trade rules. Wherever export controls stop at the first invoice, middlemen bridge the rest.

Back in Washington, market columnist Charlie Garcia (Capital Mischief) posed two uncomfortable questions about how the scandal surfaced: why did a file that sat inside the government for roughly 13 months leak in two distinct waves, and why, according to press reports, did nobody warn Huang? Cross-checked against the original publication dates, Wave 1 broke on October 29, 2026, when the Times revealed U.S. intelligence viewed Huang as a Chinese spy target—days after NVIDIA lobbied chip curbs out of the defense bill—then went silent across the November 3 midterms before Wave 2 ran daily from November 5 through November 11: House briefing demands (Nov. 5), Times and Financial Times Ministry of State Security memo leaks (Nov. 6–7), Rubio’s spring memo (Nov. 8), the BIS intervention (Nov. 9), The Information (Nov. 10), and the direct-report affair and board ouster leaks (Nov. 11, two days past Garcia’s Nov. 9 cutoff). Analysts such as Doomberg, quoted by Garcia, called the rollout “a coordinated hit” from Washington ahead of U.S.–China summit talks—a political-targeting theory echoed from Beijing’s angle by state-run Global Times—whereas Garcia himself attributed the two waves to competing administration factions: State and NSC China hawks around Rubio’s shop opening the door on policy in late October, followed after Election Day by subpoena-wary White House aides and sidelined BIS agents emptying the drawer.

Garcia’s second question cuts just as deep. As the Times and subsequent reports noted, U.S. counterintelligence watched Alice Huang for roughly a year and briefed the White House repeatedly without ever giving Jensen Huang a standard “defensive briefing”—whether to avoid tipping off an active surveillance target, because White House inaction froze agencies down the chain, or, as Garcia asks, because an unwarned CEO remained useful leverage.

Whatever Washington’s motive, the boardroom endgame moved fast. Even after NVIDIA’s board said on November 8 that it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised—and brought in Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz on November 10—Wednesday’s reports that directors were preparing to oust Huang sent the stock down more than 8 percent in November 11 after-hours trading. On Thursday, November 12, NVIDIA announced—and filed in an SEC Form 8-K—that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive officer, and board member effective immediately, ending his 33-year run. The board appointed longtime executive vice president and chief financial officer Colette Kress as interim CEO while she retains her CFO role, and named Harvey C. Jones its first-ever chair.

Meanwhile, Wall Street spent Wednesday evening, November 11, dumping NVIDIA. On Thursday, November 19, if the IPO schedule holds, it gets to buy Anthropic. Read Anthropic’s November 10 Form S-1/A prospectus before calling that a change of heart. At the assumed $74.00 midpoint of its $70-to-$78 offering range, NVIDIA itself has agreed to buy 135,135,135 shares of non-voting Class N stock—about $10 billion—in a concurrent private placement contingent on and closing immediately after the IPO. Jensen Huang is out the door, and the CFO who kept the books now sits in his chair too. Somebody still has to sign the check.
